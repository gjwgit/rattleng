ABSTRACT

1

Knowledge Graphs (KGs) form the backbone of many knowledge
dependent applications such as search engines, digital personal
assistants, and question-answering machines. KGs are constructed
either manually or automatically using a variety of extraction techniques applied over multiple data sources. As we cannot guarantee
the accuracy of these data sources, and due to the expenses and
difficulties involved in data validation prior to KG formation, it is
highly likely that there are incorrect and missing triples in a KG,
and it is unrealistic to expect a perfectly accurate and complete
archive of knowledge. Therefore, a KG will have a trade-off between
its coverage and correctness. To address this issue of KG validation,
and to identify interesting and unusual triples that are worth investigating, we introduce SEKA, a novel unsupervised approach to
detect anomalous entities (nodes) and triples in a KG. SEKA can
help improve the correctness of a KG whilst retaining its coverage.
We propose an adaption of the Path Rank Algorithm (PRA), named
Corroborative Path Rank Algorithm (CPRA), which is a novel and
efficient adaptation of PRA that is customized to detect anomalies in
KGs. With the use of a one-class Support Vector Machine (SVM), we
identify abnormal entities and triples in an unsupervised manner
without any user involvement or dependency on external sources.
We evaluate our approach using the four real world KGs YAGO-1,
KBpedia, Wikidata, and DSKG to demonstrate the ability of our
approach in outperforming a baseline approach both in terms of
run time and quality of the results. Furthermore, we conduct Knowledge Graph Completion (KGC) over three benchmark KGs FB15K,
FB15K-237, and WN18, to demonstrate how anomaly detection can
supplement a downstream task such as KGC.

Knowledge Graphs (KGs) are now the backbone of many information systems that require access to structured knowledge [12].
Even though the phrase `Knowledge Graph' has been used in the
literature since early 1970s, the modern incarnation occurred since
the announcement made by Google in 2012, followed by the adoption of KGs by other industrial giants such as Amazon, eBay, and
Facebook [21]. Furthermore, the introduction of linked open data
contributed to shifting current research interest towards KGs [12].
Many of the KGs published as linked open data have been created from semi-structured or unstructured data sources [17, 30].
The sizes of KGs such as YAGO [14] and DBpedia [4] make manual
curation difficult, thus requiring the use of heuristics for knowledge
extraction given a context. However, these heuristics cannot guarantee that a KG is free from anomalies due to the lack of human
validation [46]. Linked data which is extracted from semi-structured
and unstructured sources is likely to contain anomalies in the form
of incorrect RDF (Resource Description Framework) statements
and incomplete facts [5, 36]. For example, Wikipedia, which is used
as a data source in constructing YAGO and DBpedia, is estimated
to contain 2.8% wrong triples [48]. Hence, automatic approaches to
detect anomalous triples in a KG are important for the improvement
of KG quality [30]. However, it remains a challenging task to detect
anomalies on real world KGs, due to their size and their unique
data characteristics, including directed triples, relations of different
types, and semantic properties [28], thus requiring a more scalable
and practical approach for anomaly detection.
Anomaly or outlier detection is the process of identifying unusual patterns or rare occurrences that deviate from the majority in
a dataset [3]. In the context of KGs, anomaly detection is important
due to its ability to improve the quality of a KG [40] by identifying
entities or triples that deviate from the majority due to an associated
anomaly. For a given KG, performing anomaly detection as the first
step in the cleaning process will provide a much cleansed KG as an
input to validation and enrichment methods, thereby improving
their performance in terms of quality.
In light of these challenges involving the quality enhancement
of KGs, we propose SEKA, an unsupervised anomaly detection approach to identify anomalous entities, and triples in a KG using both
structural characteristics of a graph and content of graph's nodes.
In this paper, we will be using the intuitive notion of an anomaly as
being interesting or unusual such as semantically incorrect triples,
contradicting triples, triples with redundant information, triples
with invalid/missing content, and entities with contradicting type
information. Also, we use the term entity to refer to a node in the
KG that represents a real world object (such as a person, movie,
dataset, and so on), and we interchangeably use the terms fact and
triple to represent two related entities.

SEKA adopts a variation of the traditional Path Rank Algorithm
(PRA), the Corroborative Path Rank Algorithm (CPRA) which we
introduce as a novel customization of PRA to achieve the task of
anomaly detection. CPRA considers all corroborative (alternative)
paths between two entities in a fact (triple) bounded by a specific
length to construct binary features. While we consider these paths
as features, we mark the existence or non-existence of a path between two entities with a binary True/False value, thus generating
binary features. To reduce complexity and to improve performance,
CPRA adopts a depth-first search bounded by length as opposed to
random walks. Using CPRA, we construct a binary feature vector
for each triple selected in the KG thus forming a matrix of features.
We then prune the feature space for improved performance. We
train a one-class Support Vector Machine [38] to perform unsupervised anomaly detection on the feature matrix [8]. Once we
have identified anomalies, we can validate them either manually
with the involvement of domain experts, or we can remove the
identified abnormal triples from the KG and use the refined graph
in a downstream task such as Knowledge Graph Completion (KGC).
To evaluate our approach, we conduct several experiments using
two sets of KGs. First, we evaluate our approach over the four real
world KGs YAGO-1, KBpedia, Wikidata, and DSKG. We selected
these KGs to demonstrate the applicability of our approach on KGs
from different domains of varying sizes. We introduce INK (INject
anomalies to Knowledge graphs) to inject synthetic anomalies to
these KGs due to the absence of labeled data (ground truth anomalies). Using these four KGs, we evaluate SEKA without CPRA, but
with the traditional PRA [26] which we consider as the baseline.
Furthermore, we conduct experiments to evaluate the performance
of SEKA in identifying anomalies under the three aforementioned
tasks. Second, we evaluate our approach on the three benchmark
datasets FB15K, FB15K-237, and WN18, by performing KGC to
demonstrate how well our approach helps in improving the quality
of KGC results. Third, we also conduct a manual review of the top
most abnormal triples identified from YAGO-1, KBpedia, Wikidata,
and DSKG.

RELATED WORK

We now review related work in the domains of unsupervised anomaly detection, and anomaly detection in graphs and KGs,
To handle the problem of anomaly detection, there have been
many techniques developed in the past decade, especially for detecting outliers and anomalies in unstructured data [2, 13, 23]. However,
in the real world, data objects are often related to each other and
exhibit relationships. In fact, most relational data can be considered
as inter-dependent, which requires the consideration of related objects when aiming to identify anomalies [11]. Hence, using graphs
for anomaly detection has become increasingly popular [3, 29].
Anomaly detection in graphs can be performed using either supervised, unsupervised, or semi-supervised techniques [20]. However, there is no universally accepted single outlier detection approach, as the choice of a suitable approach depends on the nature
of the problem, and the availability of data and their attributes. In
the recent past, unsupervised anomaly detection techniques have
become prominent among researchers [6, 18, 37], due to the cost
involved in both obtaining labelled data, and depending on external
resources for supervised learning [13]. Specific techniques have
been introduced to detect anomalies in KGs as existing graph-based
anomaly detection approaches only consider structural properties,
dependent on the graph type, and are not scalable to handle large
graphs such as KGs [40].
Anomaly detection in KGs has received much attention as automated methods of constructing KGs prioritize data integrity [19].
There are a few methods for error detection in KGs, where each
approach may target specific types of information [35]. There are
also approaches taking advantage of entity type information to perform clustering-based outlier detection [9]. However, entity types
can either be absent or only partially available in real world KGs.
Alternatively, another set of approaches use path ranking [30], and
path-based rule mining for error checking [41]. While path ranking
methods have limitations in the coverage, path-based rule mining
methods have limitations in the quality of the rules.
Most of the recent studies propose to employ supervised classifiers to evaluate every triple based on different features, including entity categories, path features, in/out-degrees, as well as embedding representations of entities and relations [30]. However,
ground truth data may not be available to train such classifiers.
Alternatively, there have been efforts to utilize external information sources [10, 22, 27, 32, 47], such as related web pages [27] and
annotations [24, 28], to facilitate anomaly detection. While having
external resources can be valuable for this task, acquiring such

UNSUPERVISED ANOMALY DETECTION

Following recent work in anomaly detection in KGs [25, 40, 43, 52],
our aim is to discover abnormal triples and entities that provide

contradicting, semantically incorrect, redundant information, or
contain invalid, incomplete, missing information.
Our approach can perform three anomaly detection tasks as
shown in Figure 1. (1) General fact anomaly detection considers all
triples in the entire graph and aims to identify anomalous triples. (2)
Similarly, the task of identifying anomalous target predicate facts
aims to identify abnormal triples within a given target predicate.
For example, find anomalous triples among the triples with bornIn
predicate. While this task is similar in execution to the first task
of anomaly detection, its goal is to find anomalies related to a
specific group of triples without considering the entire graph, thus
achieving an advantage over the run time. (3) The third task is to
identify anomalous entities given a particular entity type. That is,
we identify abnormal entities considering both structural properties

While most of the KGs provide entity type information, we
introduced ENTGENE (ENtity Type GENErator), which operates
independently from other external KGs to generate or infer the
entity types in their absence. If the entity type information is absent
in the KG, we use Named Entity Recognition (NER) in Natural
Language Processing (NLP) [31] to generate the type information
with the use of Stanford2 and Spacy3 libraries in Python. If the
type information is partially available in the KG or when the type
information cannot be generated (identified as OTHER by NER),
ENTGENE infers the type using the type information of already
identified entities. For example, if the majority of objects    in
triples with the predicate livesIn have the type LOCATION, we make
this inference for entities with missing type information, where
the object of a triple with the predicate livesIn should be of type
LOCATION. While it is possible to have multiple types associated
with an entity due to inferencing, any abnormalities related to entity
type information will be identified during the process of target type
entity anomaly detection.

vector machine (SVM) classifier can be used to learn the decision
boundary based only on a set of data points and their distribution in
an unsupervised manner [38]. The one-class -SVM was originally
developed to identify dense areas of high-dimensional distributions.
Data points are mapped into a feature space such that a given set

3.3

Validation of anomalies

From the output of the -SVM, we can extract the abnormal triples
or entities and validate them manually with insights from a domain
expert. This manual validation can aid in correcting the identified
anomalies to improve KG quality. Furthermore, we can remove
these anomalous triples from the KG and subject the refined KG for
a downstream task such as Knowledge Graph Completion (KGC)
to observe performance improvement.

Parameter Settings

We now discuss the parameter settings used in SEKA. To eliminate
the need of having user involvement during the anomaly detection
process, our approach is designed in such a way that it has a minimal
number of parameters that need to be manually set.
In the step of constructing features as explained in Section 3.1.1,
to determine the path length to traverse in depth-first search, we
use  where it is by default set as  = 0.5, 1, 2. Hence, no user
involvement is required to determine the value of .
In the step of identifying anomalies, the -SVM requires configurations for the two parameters, its kernel and . In order to obtain
robust results, we train four -SVMs with the four kernels RBF,
poly, linear, and sigmoid. This ensures our classification outcomes
are not biased due to the use of a single kernel function. Hence, the
user does not have to configure the kernel for training. The ratio
of normal to abnormal classified feature vectors can be specified

hasPredecessor and hasSuccessor with an object of type PERSON.

the composer and album as one entity.

which are not abnormal independently, but contradicting when
considered as a pair.

hasLabel, "">, <-n-a, hasDefinition, "">, and <-n-a, hasLabel, ""> have
an empty object. Furthermore, the following triples <12 - Dimethoxybenzene, hasLabel, "Veratrol"> and <12 - Dimethoxybenzene, hasLabel,
"Veratrole"> provide redundant information due to minor variations
in the spellings.
In DSKG, the triple <person/11203, hasName, A.> is anomalous
due to the abnormality in the object. While the triple should represent the name of the person identified by the number 11203, the
name provided is a single letter which causes the anomaly. While

most of the datasets have a single website to provide more information about the dataset known as the landing page, the entity
dataset/1 in DSKG has three landing pages. Even though the three
triples related to this dataset have no anomaly in nature, they were
identified as abnormal triples since this is the only dataset with
three landing pages out of which one is a broken link. Similarly,
the dataset dataset/411 has the highest number of creators, which
is thirteen. None of the other datasets have this many creators thus
making the triples related to dataset/411 abnormal.
Similar to KBpedia, Wikidata does not have many structural
anomalies. Most of the anomalies are associated with the literals.
The structural anomalies are related to entities rich in information
(triples) as opposed to the majority with a limited amount of information. For example, the entity L10551 has five triples associated
with it, where as other entities have at most two triples. While the
triples itself are not abnormal, the scenario of one entity having
many facts compared to the rest is anomalous. In wikidata, literal
based anomalies are mostly due to absence of values, or presence
of a numerical value when a string is required. For example, the
entity L158675-F1 has an empty value for its label.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

In this paper, we have present an approach to study the problem of
discovering anomalous triples and entities in a Knowledge Graph
(KG) in an unsupervised manner. Our approach can identify anomalies related to both structure and content of the KG, it is KG independent, and has the ability to identify a multitude of anomalies
without depending on any external sources. A triple is considered
anomalous if it is semantically incorrect, contradicting, has incorrect entity type information, provides redundant information, or
has an invalid/missing literal.
We conducted six different experiments using four real world
KGs with synthetic anomalies, and three benchmark KGs to demonstrate the state-of-the-art performance of our approach in anomaly
detection. The results of the experimental evaluation show an increase of up to 16% in both precision and recall, and a decrease in
the run time of up to 23% in comparison with the baseline. Furthermore, the results show how KG refinement via anomaly detection
can improve the quality of KGC. Subjecting a cleansed KG for KGC
after performing anomaly detection as opposed to performing KGC
without any cleaning has an increase in Hits@10 of up to 13% and
an increase in MRR of up to 11%.
As for future work, we aim to generalize the identified anomalies
with the use of a constraint-based language such as SHACL [33].
Once we generalize anomalies, we can use them as validations
during the process of KG construction to filter triples. Also we aim
to conduct more baseline experiments with existing graph anomaly
detection techniques.
